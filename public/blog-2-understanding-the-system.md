## Understanding Cutoffs and Good Attempts

Every serious CAT aspirant eventually asks two questions:

- What percentile do I need for top B-schools?
- How many questions should I attempt in the actual exam?

The answer lies in understanding performance benchmarks - specifically **cutoff trends** and **good attempts analysis**. When interpreted correctly, these benchmarks provide clarity, direction, and realistic goal-setting.

## Understanding CAT Cutoff Trends (IIMs & Leading B-Schools)

CAT cutoffs operate at two levels:

1. **Sectional Cutoffs**
2. **Overall Percentile Cutoffs**

Both play an important role in the shortlisting process.

### Sectional Cutoffs

Most IIMs require candidates to clear minimum percentile thresholds in each section - VARC, DILR, and QA.

For General category candidates at older IIMs, sectional cutoffs generally fall in the **80–85 percentile range**, serving as the minimum qualifying benchmark, though exact requirements vary by institute.

Balanced performance across all sections therefore becomes essential.

### Overall Cutoff Trends — IIMs

For top-tier IIMs, the overall percentile typically is required to be in the range of:

- **99+ percentile** for strong shortlisting probability
- **98+ percentile** depending on profile strength

For other established IIMs:

- **95–98 percentile** range often becomes competitive

For newer IIMs:

- **90–95 percentile** range may be competitive depending on profile and category

Cutoffs evolve each year based on:

- Number of applicants
- Score distribution
- Institute selection criteria
- Academic diversity considerations

### Cutoffs — Leading Non-IIM Institutes

Several leading B-schools also accept CAT scores, with competitive benchmarks such as:

- **95+ percentile** for institutes like MDI, SPJIMR (profile-based evaluation may apply)
- **90–95 percentile** for many reputed private institutions

Each institute uses a composite evaluation system combining CAT percentile, academics, work experience, and interview performance.

## Good Attempts Analysis — How Many Questions Should You Attempt?

While percentile drives admissions, the exam is ultimately attempted question by question. This is where a good attempt strategy becomes crucial.

"Good attempts" refers to attempting the right questions with high accuracy.

### What Is Considered a Good Attempt?

In a moderately challenging year (68-question format):

**For 99 Percentile (Approximate Benchmark)**

- 40–45 high-quality attempts
- Strong accuracy (80%+)

**For 95 Percentile**

- 30–35 well-selected attempts
- Strong accuracy (80%+)

**For 90 Percentile**

- 25–30 structured attempts
- Balanced performance across sections

These numbers vary depending on difficulty level and score distribution, but they provide directional clarity.

### Section-Wise Good Attempts (Moderate Year Reference)

**VARC (24 Questions)**

- 16–18 thoughtful attempts for high percentile range

**DILR (22 Questions)**

- 10–14 attempts depending on set selection

**QA (22 Questions)**

- 12–16 attempts with accuracy focus

## A Tactical Insight: Sectional Distribution Matters More Than Equal Division

A common misconception among aspirants is that a 90-mark score automatically implies a 30–30–30 split across VARC, DILR, and QA.

In reality, CAT rewards sectional strength combined with balance above cutoff levels.

For example, a candidate scoring:

- 35 in VARC
- 20 in DILR
- 35 in QA

may still comfortably reach a high overall percentile, provided sectional cutoffs are cleared.

What matters is:

- Clearing sectional minimum thresholds in weaker areas
- Capitalizing on strengths
- Maintaining overall score momentum

The exam allows room for strategic variation. Strong performance in two sections can meaningfully compensate for a moderate performance in another — as long as sectional requirements are met.

This reinforces an important preparation principle: the objective is not to divide effort equally, but to **optimise performance intelligently**.

## What These Benchmarks Actually Mean

Performance benchmarks aren't rigid rules. They serve as preparation targets.

They help aspirants:

- Set realistic mock goals
- Track improvement objectively
- Align attempts with percentile ambitions
- Develop section-wise strategies

The key is **consistency in mock performance** rather than isolated high scores.

## Final Thought

Understanding cutoffs and good attempts transforms preparation from reactive to strategic. When aspirants know what percentile they are targeting and what level of attempt quality supports that goal, preparation becomes focused and measurable.

Clear benchmarks lead to structured improvement — and structured improvement leads to stronger outcomes.
